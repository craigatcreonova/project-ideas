# Point in time snapshot of ideas about early startups

*Source: six Medium posts (Apr-May 2023). The ideas and operating principles, separated from the business plan.*

## 1. Building in the open

**Why:**
- The market segment is early and dynamic, and the subject is one the author is still learning, so input and structuring thoughts in public help disproportionately.
- Awareness: partners, employees, and early advisors are valuable, and openness lowers barriers to reaching them.
- Low cost of leaking information early: ideas are easy, execution is hard, and startups can't "appear larger than they are" as much as founders think.
- Culture: a company that builds its plan in the open is more likely to make other open choices later (e.g. public salary bands).
- Social commitment and accountability create progress.

**Related habits:** default-open Slack channels; Creative Commons licensing of competencies on GitHub.

**Corollary:** you don't preserve your dignity by waiting to announce until you know it worked.

## 2. Startup strategy principles

- **Problems over ideas.** Startups can act without delay and take risks that mature companies can't, which suits problem spaces. Ideas are still easier to generate and communicate, and a rough visual beats a precise but abstract description.
- **A random trigger is fine.** You don't need a billion-dollar idea, only preference, excitement, and curiosity.
- **Turn the idea into a market opportunity.** Map trends and ask why it might win, both to reason about potential and to communicate.
- **Reason from contingent trends.** Ask "what needs to be true for outcome X?" instead of requiring proof.
- **Start from specific ideas to manage breadth,** even though that's imperfect experimental design. Resolve the bias in later steps.
- **Progressive de-risking.** A startup is a sequence of learning steps that can send you back to restart, refine, or move forward.
- **Expect to change the idea,** and expect a lot of work (models, research, interviews) before it's specific enough to engage others and to convince yourself it can work.
- **Check fundamental assumptions,** even when you already know to stay close to the customer.
- **Do things that don't scale** to test the real system early.
- **Advantage of startups:** avoiding inertia, not volume of work. Goals should feel very near.
- **Specific goals matter.** Define what success at the end of the sprint looks like.
- **Try unconventional life and process hacks** if they move you forward, even with some risk.

## 3. Learning and framework use

- Make intentional space to be curious. The ideas came out of having room to make connections.
- Learn through "applied conceptual" hypotheticals: what could this be used for, what's needed, who needs these properties.
- Use structured tools (Lean Canvas, Where to Play / How to Win) informally as prompts, and formally only when they help align or communicate with others.

## 4. Dealing with randomness and incomplete information

- Randomness plays a bigger role than people assume, in both directions. Entrepreneurship mostly makes risk visible, since "safe" choices have hidden downsides.
- Acknowledge the birth lottery without becoming fatalistic and without dismissing others' accomplishments.
- **Directed opportunism** (*The Art of Action*; a submarine leadership video): be highly proficient at routine work and keep strategic intent clear and simple, so people can act safely on changing circumstances. Example: a short-deadline accelerator application was feasible because intent and skills were already in place.
- **Thinking in bets:** ask "how should my actions change based on this outcome or new information?"
- **Decision review:** analyze wins and losses with a confusion matrix (including decisions not taken), plus a "did we get the theory of the outcome right?" axis. Don't reward correct outcomes reached for the wrong reasons.

## 5. Decision rhythm and operating modes

- **Take a bearing.** Alternate between moving in a planned direction and pausing to reset direction based on where you actually ended up. During the pause, rebuild the mental model, and don't push the plan further or gather more data. The two modes each get full focus.
- **Be deliberate about decisions** and avoid "backing into" a situation.
- **Define outcomes and exit conditions for each phase or mode.** Otherwise you can just taper off. Nurture mode did this with three monitored outcome areas.
- **Some processes just take time.** You can't grow a tree ten times faster by planting ten trees. IRL customer development and organism engineering run on longer cycles than digital work.

## 6. Getting advice and input

- Conversations with people who ask good questions are valuable. Prepare a few focus areas and some open-ended prompts.
- Outsiders won't follow your structure and will bring alternate framings. Impose structure on the feedback yourself and steer toward the useful parts.
- Choose deliberately how to respond to a signal.

## 7. Team and culture

- **Leaders own high-level direction,** including what *not* to prioritize. "Servant leadership" shouldn't become abdication. Leaders can still take input, be humble, and let people experiment without approval. What counts as "high level" changes with the org's stage.
- **Early on, a plan helps people get on the bus,** but the plan is also the leader's responsibility in its own right.
- **Hire for "energy giving," not "fit."** Fit works as a veto and hurts diversity. Energy giving is a learnable skill: conveying respect, owning contributions, solving together, committing to try solutions. It lets very different working styles coexist. Avoid toxic positivity.
- **Value diverse backgrounds and ways of thinking,** and turn them into an effective group (the recent Properly teams are the model).
- **There's no single right cofounder:** a wide set of people could work, so go wide.
- **Public salary bands and similar openness** are cultural bets that early openness makes easier.

## 8. Networking and outreach

- Prefer one-on-one conversations over large events.
- Start from where the people are. Lead with "what can I offer or share?" and not only "what can I get?"
- Accept that timing may not suit people, and that this is fine.
- Be comfortable reaching out broadly, and use tooling (a free CRM) to run a tight process and not go dark on contacts.
- Use interested-but-unavailable people to point you to others.
