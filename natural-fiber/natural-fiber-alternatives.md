# Natural Fibers, Human Scale: Options to Explore

## Foreword

This document sketches a handful of possible business directions built around natural fibers in Nova Scotia. Its purpose is to gather input from reviewers: which directions deserve more thought, what is missing, and which assumptions look wrong.

The common thread is human scale. The region has small flocks, a strong craft and alternative-living community, and a seasonal tourism economy, and each option below tries to fit that scale rather than compete with industrial supply chains. Everything here is a hypothesis to test, not a plan.

The projects below require the people involved to collectively cover investment, fabric craft, and local knowledge, and in some cases fashion design and online commerce.

## Context

- **Plenty of fiber animals, little fiber.** Sheep are raised in the region mostly for meat rather than fiber, and alpacas and other fiber animals are also present. Fleece often has low value because there is no easy way to process it, so it drops out of the chain before carding, spinning, and fabric.
- **Tourism with an appetite for experiences.** Summer visitors look for both activities and products to take home.
- **An active community.** Crafts, alternative living, the Irish language, and a culture of doing things together.
- **Traditional crafts are in demand elsewhere.** In urban centers, spinning and quilting are popular experiences, particularly among affluent hobbyists. The same appeal could be offered locally, to visitors and residents.

## The idea map

| Approach | Who might use it? (hypothesis) | Scale | Margin | Confidence | What it could look like |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Fleece processing for small farms** | Hobby farmers and human-scale flocks with fleeces and no easy next step. (Large operations likely already have solutions.) | Low | Medium | High. Fleece is close to a byproduct, so farmers have little to lose and may welcome cost recovery or a way to use the whole animal. | *Scene 1:* A farmer drops off fleeces and gets their own yarn back. *Scene 2:* The business buys the fleece outright and sells the finished yarn. |
| **Craft experiences** | Visitors on vacation, locals, and hobbyists drawn to traditional crafts. | Medium, and seasonal. The tourist side drives it; the local side is probably capped at low. | Depends on the market (see note 1) | High, but it takes time to build. | *Scene 1, visitors:* A pop-up spinning evening in a community hall or local business, with no fixed infrastructure. A group learns carding and spinning and takes home their first yarn. Fixed price or pay what you can. *Scene 2, locals:* A pop-up that might run on a regular schedule, at a hall, a business, or a neighbor's venue. Pay what you can or barter. |
| **Garment line: local channel** | Visitors and locals buying from shops around the region. | Medium | High if carefully targeted (see note 2) | Medium. Reputation and word of mouth build in naturally at the local level. | *Scene:* A shopper in a village store finds a wool garment and learns where the fiber came from. Comparable: a local cobbler whose durable, high-priced footwear stays busy. |
| **Garment line: local plus online** | The same people, plus anyone who sees the product on vacation and decides later at home. | High | High if carefully targeted, but riskier (see note 2) | Medium. Online has to work harder, through careful targeting, to earn the trust that local reputation provides. | *Scene:* A visitor sees the pieces in a shop, thinks it over after the trip, and orders from home. |

**Note 1, experiences margin:** Visitors and locals are different markets, and the business would likely want different things from each. Pay what you can is expected to land differently for each group: probably higher from tourists, lower from locals, with barter as a local option. Is there a pricing model that handles both well? Open question.

**Note 2, garment margin:** Handmade natural fiber garments with real labor in them sit in a very different pricing world from fast fashion. The model works when the audience is tightly targeted. Online makes the product feel more comparable to everything else, even when it is distinctive, so that challenge is sharper there.

The two garment rows are one idea, end to end from local fiber to finished clothing with a traceable story. They differ only in sales channel. Separating the travel moment from the purchase decision could also widen the audience.

## The process

Raw fleece, then carding (into roving or batts), then spinning into yarn, then weaving or knitting into fabric, then garments. Each step is a possible entry point, and the fleece stage is where value currently drops away.

## Next steps

The proposed next step is for each reviewer to reason about the options against three lenses:

1. **User need.** Which people or needs does each option serve best?
2. **Opportunity.** How large and durable does each option look, given the scale, margin, and confidence ratings above?
3. **Enthusiasm.** Which options most excite the people who would be involved in building them?

The decision point, meaning how and when to narrow the options, is left open for now.
