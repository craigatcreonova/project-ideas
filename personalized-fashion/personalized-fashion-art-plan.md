# Personalized Fashion & Art Marketplace: Planning Artifact

**Stage:** Exploration. This document keeps every path open. For each choice it describes what the path looks like, what would need to be true, and how it shapes the resulting business. Economics is one lens among several; culture, mission, and employment carry equal weight.

---

## 1. The Idea in One Paragraph

A marketplace where a customer records a short video selfie at home (a twirl, arm lifts), and receives a precise body model. They then choose from, or mash up, designs made by a community of designers and get clothing that is perfectly fitted and entirely theirs. Designers (including hobbyist and game-based designers) are compensated for their creative work. Production is a deliberate open choice among human craft, a gig sewing network, and automation.

---

## 2. The User Experience (Customer Side)

1. **Capture.** At home, in private, in tight-fitting clothes, record a video selfie (twirl, lift arms).
2. **Design.** Select from existing designs or mash several together.
3. **Result.** Perfectly fitted clothing that is entirely theirs.

**Design note:** Doing this in the privacy of one's own home is part of the premise, not an incidental detail. It affects trust, data handling, and who is willing to try it at all.

---

## 3. Foundations

### Inspiration
- **Micro-transaction creative marketplaces** (Inkbox, Teachers Pay Teachers) that widen participation in the creative economy.
  - *Global reach:* individuals anywhere can participate.
  - *Sector reach:* part-time artists with day jobs elsewhere can earn without going full-time.

### What you would need to believe
There is real, paying demand for personalized clothing, driven by at least one of two distinct motives:

| Motive | Description | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Unique expression** | Desire for one-of-a-kind pieces | "One of a kind shows" |
| **Particular function** | Desire for clothing that solves a practical problem | Sizing that actually fits, fashion-forward clothes that still have pockets |

These may be different customers, price points, and business models. The plan should stay open to serving one, the other, or both.

### Enabling trend
- **Image processing and depth sensing in everyone's pocket.** From the Kinect era to LiDAR in consumer smartphones, 3D capture is now commodity hardware.

---

## 4. The Background Layer (Sizing Technology)

| Layer | What it does | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Core pipeline** | Processes video frames into precise body measurements | Potentially borrows from 3D modelling and from the motion capture used in games and films |
| **Second data layer** | Adds LiDAR depth data when the phone has it | Improves dimensional accuracy |
| **Fallback (no LiDAR)** | Include an object of known size in frame (e.g. a held-up coin) | Gives a scale reference; widens reach to older or cheaper phones |

**What would need to be true:** video-derived measurements are accurate enough to produce garments that fit without a fitting session, and customers trust the process enough to wear fitted clothing on camera, even if only privately.

**Impact threads:** body data is sensitive (storage, deletion, who can see it); inclusive sizing across body types is both a mission opportunity and a technical test.

---

## 5. The Design Side of the Marketplace

**The premise:** Designing clothes is satisfying enough that an entire genre of fashion games exists around it, some of them engaging with design realistically. That creative activity could be tapped and compensated.

**Supply sources to explore**
- Part-time and professional artists and designers (the Inkbox / Teachers Pay Teachers model).
- Fashion-game players and hobbyists who already design for fun.
- Customers themselves, via the mashup feature (customers as co-designers).

**What would need to be true**
- Designs made in a game-like or simplified tool can be translated into garments that can actually be produced.
- Compensation is meaningful enough to attract participants but sustainable at one-off volumes.
- Quality and originality can be maintained without becoming a gatekeeper that shuts out the wide participation that motivates the idea.

**Questions to leave open**
- How is compensation structured (per sale, royalty on mashups, tiered)?
- How are mashups credited and paid when several designers contribute?
- How are IP and copying handled?

**Impact threads:** a new income channel for people outside traditional fashion; cultural value of democratizing design; risk of undervaluing creative labour if payouts are tiny.

## 6. The Production and Supply Side: Three Paths

Small-quantity, made-to-measure work is the central cost challenge. Three routes are on the table. None is eliminated, and hybrids or sequencing (starting on one and migrating to another) remain possible.

### Path A: Intensive Human Labour (craft-based)

- **What it looks like:** Skilled makers produce each garment; costs are high and reflected directly in price.
- **Positioning:** Quality and durability. Buy once, replace rarely. Reference point: Patagonia-style lifetime repair guarantees.
- **What would need to be true:** Enough customers will pay a premium for fit plus durability; repair and remake services are operationally feasible; the lifetime guarantee doesn't erode margins.
- **How it shapes the business:** Lower volume, higher price, strong brand identity, repair as a service line, and a slower path to scale.
- **Impact:** Good skilled employment, preserves craft, strong mission story. Limited by how many people can afford it.

### Path B: Supply Chain Optimization (gig sewing, three-sided marketplace)

- **What it looks like:** Customers, designers, and sewers are all marketplace participants. Sewers take on jobs as gig or part-time work, extending the part-time participation theme to production.
- **What would need to be true:** Enough distributed sewing skill and capacity exists; quality control and consistent fit across many makers are achievable; logistics (pattern delivery, fabric sourcing, shipping) work at small quantities.
- **How it shapes the business:** Network effects across three sides, more complex operations, and a quality assurance layer as a core competency.
- **Impact:** Flexible income for skilled people (including those who can't work in factories), but the gig model raises questions of fair pay, stability, and benefits.

### Path C: Technology and Automation

- **What it looks like:** Automated cutting, assembly, or other production steps bring the cost of one-offs down.
- **What would need to be true:** Automation can handle the variety and one-off nature of the work; the technical problem (likely the hardest in the business) is solvable on a workable timeline and budget.
- **How it shapes the business:** Highest technical risk and capital need, but potentially the largest competitive moat, and possibly transformative economically and culturally.
- **Impact:** Could reduce dependence on labour entirely, which changes the employment story. Public views on AI and automation are mixed, which is a consideration for how it is communicated.

### Labour-Story Positioning Options

The brand story and the production method are separate choices. A few combinations to explore:

| Positioning | Description | Pairs naturally with |
|---|---|---|
| **Living wage, no sweatshop** | Competes on ethical labour credentials | Path A, Path B |
| **Global neighbours** | Costs below the domestic seller's market, but pays roughly 90th-percentile compensation in an offshore market (a living wage for global neighbours) | Path B |
| **Automation, de-emphasized** | Production process isn't the consumer headline; the product and fit are | Path C |

## 7. Choices Side by Side

| Dimension | A: Human craft | B: Gig sewing marketplace | C: Automation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost structure | High per unit | Moderate, variable | High upfront, low marginal (if solved) |
| Scale path | Slow | Network-driven | Technology-driven |
| Main risk | Market size at premium price | Quality consistency, fair labour | Technical feasibility |
| Brand promise | Lasting quality | Participation, fit, fair pay | Fit and personalization |
| Employment effect | Skilled, stable jobs | Flexible, distributed work | Fewer production jobs |
| Cultural effect | Craft revival | Creative and maker community | Technological shift |
| Potential moat | Brand and trust | Network | Technology |

## 8. Impact Lens (Applies to Every Path)

- **Culture:** Who gets to participate in fashion and design? Does it widen access to self-expression and clothing that fits everyone?
- **Mission:** Fit for underserved bodies and needs (e.g. pockets, sizing); longevity over disposability.
- **Employment:** Who gets work, on what terms, and how does that change as the business scales or automates?
- **Privacy and trust:** Body data stewardship is part of the mission, not just compliance.

---

## 9. Key Assumptions to Test

1. Customers want personalized clothing enough to pay for it (and which motive, expression or function, is stronger).
2. Video-based measurement can deliver a reliably good fit, with and without LiDAR.
3. Customers are comfortable with the private-home capture experience and with how their body data is handled.
4. Hobbyist and game designers can produce designs that translate to real garments, and will participate if compensated.
5. At least one production path can be run at small quantities with acceptable quality and cost.
6. The chosen brand story is one the operations can honestly support.

## 10. Open Questions

- Which customer motive (expression vs function) should anchor the first version, or are both served from the start?
- Which production path is the starting point, and does the plan assume migration (for example, from human or gig production toward automation) over time?
- How would designer compensation scale with a small number of units per design?
- What is the minimum viable version that tests the fit-from-video promise with real garments?
- How is the supply of sewers or makers recruited and trained, and under what terms?

## 11. Parking Lot

Random specifics worth capturing but not yet placed in the plan. Nothing here is a commitment.

### Design lineage on the label or card
- **The idea:** The people involved in each design that was mashed up could be named on the garment's label or an accompanying card, helping the wearer connect to the designers.
- **Possible extension:** Depending on the production strategy, the same approach could credit the people who made the garment (sewers or makers in a gig or craft model).
- **Related threads elsewhere in the plan:** designer credit and compensation for mashups (Section 5), and the labour-story positioning options (Section 6).

### Tailored fashion without affluence (Ghana as an example)
- **The observation:** Culturally, unique and tailored fashion exists even in environments without a lot of affluence, with Ghana as one example. Personalized clothing is not inherently a luxury or wealthy-market behavior.
- **Why it may matter (to explore, not conclude):**
  - It bears on the "what you would need to believe" premise in Section 3: demand for expression and fit may not depend on high incomes.
  - It connects to the global reach inspiration and to the "global neighbours" production positioning in Section 6.
  - It raises the question of whether existing local tailoring traditions are customers, collaborators, a production network, or a source of designers, and how the business would relate to them rather than displace them.
- **Open questions:** What would the business offer that complements local tailoring rather than competing with it? How would pricing, payment methods, and phone capability (see the no-LiDAR fallback in Section 4) need to work in lower-income markets?

### Reference-object sizing (e.g. a held-up coin): would it work, and how accurately?
*Quick research round, November 2026. Findings are from a handful of web sources and are directional, not a validation.*

**Short answer:** The principle is sound and the coin remains a viable fallback to keep open, but I found no study that tests a held-up coin for body circumferences at garment-grade accuracy. For that use it is unvalidated, so it should be tested rather than assumed.

**Why a reference object is needed at all**
- A single 2D image or video cannot tell you absolute size on its own, because apparent size depends on both the person's real dimensions and their distance from the camera. Recovering real-world scale needs an extra constraint such as an object of known size (a long-established idea in single-view metrology).
- A 2026 empirical study of weakly calibrated monocular body measurement compared several approaches. It found a clear trade-off between how much effort the user spends on calibration and how stable the resulting circumference estimates are. The object-calibrated method added user effort and was sensitive to lighting, contrast, and clothing choices.

**What accuracy is needed (the target to beat)**
- Industry garment tolerances are commonly around ±1 cm on major measurements for knits, and roughly ±0.5 to 1.5 cm for wovens. Fit-critical points (chest, waist, hip) are often tighter.
- One industry source notes that a 1 to 2 cm measurement error can be enough to move someone a full garment size.
- Working target for a body-measurement pipeline: aim for roughly 1 cm or better on key circumferences, with ease and fabric stretch handled separately.

**What the evidence suggests about the approaches**
| Approach | What was found | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **Coin (or other small object) as scale** | Works in principle. A small object means a small pixel error becomes a larger error when scaling the whole body. Coin sizes also vary by country and design, so the app would need to know which coin it is. Standard cards and a few specific coins are better-defined references. | No body-sizing accuracy data found for a held-up coin specifically. |
| **Credit card or ID-1 card as scale** | Widely suggested as a better reference: flat, rigid, standardized (8.56 cm wide). Research has also used a personal credit card as the reference for smartphone body measurement. | Some researchers consider small reference objects inconvenient. Sources recommending the card were mostly practitioner blogs, not peer-reviewed. |
| **Known height as the scale anchor** | Research on partial-view body estimation uses the subject's height to normalize scale, which resolves the distance versus body-size ambiguity without any extra object. In one reference-free study, a 2 cm height error produced about a 1 cm waist error. | Requires the customer to enter an accurate height; self-reported height is imperfect. |
| **Reference-free 2D silhouette method** | One study reported about 95.6% average accuracy for waist, hip, and thigh circumference (aimed at pants). | Only 12 subjects (10 male, 2 female); small and not diverse. |
| **Smartphone 3D scanning (LiDAR-class)** | A 2025 systematic review of 13 studies found strong reliability and good accuracy for smartphone and tablet 3D scanning against reference methods. A 2022 study found a free app's circumference estimates were comparable to a validated 3D scanner when both were compared with trained anthropometrists. | These are mostly body-composition and research settings, not garment fitting. |

**Practical details if a reference object is used**
- Keep the camera parallel to the body, and place the object on the same plane as the body part being measured, not in front of or behind it. A patent in this area suggests holding the object right next to the body, for example at the front of the belly when posing in profile.
- A disk-shaped or ball-shaped reference has the advantage that its apparent size stays recognizable whatever its orientation, which matters for something held in a hand.
- Use a flat, rigid, standard-size object, since a random coin, key, or bottle cap introduces size variation.
- The video format helps: many frames of a twirl allow averaging, and the customer could be prompted to show the object in several poses.

**Why the fitted-clothing requirement is pragmatic**
- Research notes that mobile scanners cannot perfectly separate clothing from the body, so accuracy improves when less or tighter clothing is worn. The same research notes that people hesitate over minimal clothing because of privacy fears, which pushes them toward less accurate scans. This supports treating privacy (for example, on-device processing and no stored imagery) as a core design question rather than an afterthought.

**Ideas to test (not decisions)**
1. Compare coin, standard card, known-height input, and LiDAR against tape measurements taken by a trained person, on a diverse set of body types.
2. Measure error on the fit-critical points (chest or bust, waist, hip, and a leg or inseam measure) against the roughly 1 cm target.
3. Test the effect of lighting, clothing tightness, and phone model on each method.
4. Consider offering the fallback as a lower-confidence tier (for example, flagging a measurement for a quick re-check) so older and cheaper phones still get a path in.

**Sources consulted:** Bruess and Chong, "Body Size Measurement Using a Smartphone," *Electronics* 2021 (mdpi.com/2079-9292/10/11/1338); "An Empirical Study of Monocular Human Body Measurement Under Weak Calibration," arXiv 2601.01639; "A Simple Strategy for Body Estimation from Partial-View Images," arXiv 2404.09301; systematic review of smartphone and tablet 3D scanners, *BioMedical Engineering OnLine* 2025 (PMC12797699); Smith et al., *Obesity* 2022 (Wiley); patent WO2012066555A2; apparel tolerance guides (apparel.wiki, texorasourcing.com, adstronaut.net, sizemarker.com).
